About the Author

Let us meet Kathleen Mansfield Murry, who lived from 1888 to 1923 and wrote under the pen-name Katherine Mansfield. The textbook introduces her as a New Zealand short story writer. She left New Zealand at the age of nineteen and settled in the United Kingdom, where she gained the friendship of major writers such as D. H. Lawrence and Virginia Woolf.

Mansfield is remembered for the delicacy of her short stories. She does not always depend on loud action; instead, she shows small movements of feeling. In “The Singing Lesson,” one letter and one telegram completely change Miss Meadows’ mood, her voice, her classroom and even the music she teaches.

Her short story collections include Bliss and The Garden Party. The textbook also notes that she wrote many poems and that her collected letters were a great success.

Why this matters for this text: Mansfield’s gift is psychological subtlety. She shows how a private wound can enter a public classroom, and how a single message can turn despair into joy. Understanding Mansfield helps us see that the story is not simply about a music lesson; it is about how mood controls perception, behaviour and even art.

Before We Read

Have you ever entered class with one private worry in your heart? Maybe a message from a friend, a quarrel at home, or a fear about the future changed the way you spoke to everyone. That is exactly what happens to Miss Meadows. Her personal pain becomes the emotional weather of the classroom.

With despair – cold, sharp despair – buried deep in her heart like a wicked knife, Miss Meadows, in cap and gown and carrying a little baton, trod the cold corridors that led to the music hall.

The opening tells us everything. The corridors are cold, but the real coldness is inside Miss Meadows. The “wicked knife” is Basil’s letter, which has wounded her pride and hope.
