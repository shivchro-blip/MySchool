About the Poet

Let us meet William Wordsworth, who lived from 1770 to 1850. The textbook introduces him as a major English poet who, along with Samuel Taylor Coleridge, helped to launch the Romantic Age in English Literature through their joint publication Lyrical Ballads. This detail is important because Romantic poetry gives deep value to feeling, imagination, simple life, and Nature.

Wordsworth was Britain’s Poet Laureate from 1843 until his death. He was popularly known as a Nature Poet. This does not mean he merely described trees and flowers prettily. For Wordsworth, Nature was a living teacher. A bird, a flower, a breeze, or a quiet grove could become a moral and spiritual lesson.

The textbook names some of his famous poems: Daffodils, The Solitary Reaper, To the Cuckoo, The Tables Turned, and Lines Composed a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey. These works show his love for rural scenes, inward reflection, and the power of Nature to shape human feeling.

Why this matters for this text: In “Lines Written in Early Spring,” Wordsworth does exactly what a Nature Poet does. He sits quietly in a grove and listens to the blended music of Nature. But joy does not remain only joy. Nature’s harmony makes him think sadly about human cruelty. Understanding Wordsworth helps us see that the poem is not just scenery; it is a moral meditation.

Introduction / Before We Read

Imagine sitting quietly under trees, listening to birds, feeling a breeze, seeing flowers and tender twigs move gently. For a moment, everything feels peaceful. But suddenly, that peace makes you think of something sad: Why do human beings, unlike Nature, hurt one another? That emotional movement is the heart of this poem.

This is a lyrical reflective poem. It does not tell a story with characters. Instead, it records a moment of feeling. The poet sees Nature’s joy, then compares it with man’s sorrowful behaviour. The poem moves from sweet mood to sad thought, from flowers and birds to human grief.

I heard a thousand blended notes While in a grove I sate reclined,

The opening image is calm and musical. The poet is resting in a grove and hearing many natural sounds mixed together. The word blended tells us that Nature is harmonious; no sound fights another sound.

Themes and Literary Devices
Theme

Nature is full of joy and harmony. The blended notes, primrose tufts, periwinkle wreaths, playful birds, and budding twigs all suggest pleasure. In an essay, write that Wordsworth sees Nature not as a lifeless background, but as a living presence filled with happiness.

Theme

Man has broken away from Nature’s holy plan. The poet believes Nature links itself to the human soul, yet human beings create sorrow for one another. The repeated question “What Man has made of Man” expresses grief over cruelty, war, selfishness, and destruction.

Theme

Joy can awaken sorrow. The poet’s pleasant thoughts bring sad thoughts. This is a subtle emotional truth: sometimes a beautiful scene makes us more aware of what is wrong in human life.

Personification

And ‘tis my faith that every flower / Enjoys the air it breathes. Flowers are given the human ability to enjoy and breathe.

Rhetorical Question

Have I not reason to lament / What Man has made of Man? The poet does not expect a simple answer. He uses the question to express grief.

Personification of Nature

To her fair works did Nature link / The human soul that through me ran. Nature is treated as a living feminine power that connects with the poet’s soul.
