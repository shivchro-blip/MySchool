About the Author

Let us meet Stephen P. H. Butler Leacock, a Canadian teacher, political scientist, writer and humourist who lived from 1869 to 1944. The textbook tells us that he was educated at Upper Canada College in Toronto and at the University of Toronto, from which he graduated in 1891. He received a fellowship in Political Economy and took his Ph.D. in 1903.

Between 1915 and 1925, Leacock was the best-known English-speaking humourist in the world. This is important because his humour is not merely joking. He gently exposes people’s follies. His works include Sunshine Sketches of a Little Town, Arcadian Adventures With the Idle Rich, Economic Prosperity in the British Empire, The Dawn of Canadian History, Moonbeams From the Larger Lunacy, The Unsolved Riddle of Social Justice, My Discovery of England, and Wet, Wit and Dry Humour.

Why this matters for this text: In “With the Photographer,” Leacock makes us laugh at a photographer who believes technology can improve a human face by changing it completely. The narrator wants a likeness of himself, but the photographer wants an artistic correction. The story becomes funny, painful and sharp because it asks a serious question: what is the value of a perfect picture if it no longer resembles the person?

Before We Read

Photographs usually preserve memory. We take them at birthdays, family functions, school events, trips, festivals and important milestones because we want to freeze our favourite moments. But what happens if a photograph removes the very person it is meant to preserve?

“I want my photograph taken,” I said.

This simple opening begins the narrator’s trouble. He enters the studio hoping for an ordinary photograph, but he walks into a place where his face will be judged, twisted, corrected and finally destroyed in the name of improvement.
