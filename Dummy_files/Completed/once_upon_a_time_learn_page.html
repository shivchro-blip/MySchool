Unit 1 · Poem · Class XI General English

Once Upon a Time

Gabriel Okara · Nigerian Poet · 1921–2019
Hypocrisy Lost Innocence Father & Son Modern Society Self-Reflection

Let us begin at the beginning — with the man who wrote this poem. Gabriel Imomotimi Gbaingbain Okara was born in 1921 in Bumoundi, in the Ijaw community of what is now the Bayelsa State of Nigeria. He grew up during the twilight years of British colonial rule, in a world where African identity was constantly being measured, judged, and often dismissed against a Western standard. That experience — of being African in a colonised world, of watching your own culture being considered inferior — would shape everything he ever wrote.

Okara was educated at Government College, Umuahia, one of the most prestigious colonial schools in Nigeria, a school that also produced the great Chinua Achebe. Now, this is important. These schools were brilliant academically, but they were also schools designed to produce Africans who thought, spoke, and behaved like the English. Okara went through that system. He learned to write in English with exquisite precision. But unlike many of his contemporaries, he never forgot that he had something older and deeper inside him — an Ijaw way of seeing the world. His entire literary career is the story of that tension.

He went on to work as a bookbinder, then as a government information officer. He served as a broadcaster and journalist during the Nigerian Civil War — the brutal Biafran War of 1967 to 1970 — and saw firsthand what political ambition, tribal conflict, and the worst impulses of modern power could do to human beings. He saw smiling faces hiding knives. He saw handshakes that concealed calculation. He lived the poem we are about to study.

What made Okara's writing utterly distinctive was his method. He deliberately translated the rhythms, images, and idioms of the Ijaw language into English. His sentences feel different from standard English — they have a quality of oral storytelling, of a village elder speaking by firelight. Critics were fascinated. Some called it experimental; others called it the future of African literature. His most celebrated novel, The Voice (1964), pushed this method so far that it created its own linguistic world. Other important works include the poetry collection The Fisherman's Invocation (1978), and two beloved children's books — Little Snake and Little Frog (1981) and An Adventure to Juju Island (1992).

In 1953, his poem "The Call of the River Nun" won the best award for Literature at the Nigerian Festival of Arts — a landmark achievement that announced Okara to the wider world. He was later honoured with the Commonwealth Poetry Award, recognition of his standing in world literature. He was also one of the founding figures whose work appeared in Black Orpheus, the influential literary journal that became the home of the African literary renaissance.

Why this matters for this text

When you read "Once Upon a Time," you are not reading the abstract thoughts of a poet. You are reading the confession of a man who grew up between two worlds — the warm communal world of Ijaw village life, and the calculating, performance-driven world of colonial and post-colonial Nigeria. The smiles without eyes, the handshakes without hearts — Okara did not invent these as poetic images. He observed them. He experienced them. And then, in the most honest and courageous line of the poem, he admits that he learned to perform them too. That admission — that even a good man can be corrupted by the world around him — is what makes this poem human, and what makes it unforgettable.

Before we read a single line of this poem, I want you to do something. Think about the last time you laughed at something you actually found funny — a genuine laugh, the kind that surprises you, the kind where your whole face lights up without you deciding it should. Can you remember the last time that happened? Now think: when was the last time you smiled because you were supposed to? At a relative you barely know, at a teacher's joke that wasn't funny, at someone important whose approval you needed. Two different things, aren't they? That is exactly what this poem is about.

Gabriel Okara writes what we call a narrative monologue — a poem in which a single speaker talks directly to someone, telling them something deeply personal. The speaker is a father, and he is talking to his young son. Now, why does a father talk to his child about hypocrisy and lost innocence? Because children are the last place where honesty still lives. The child has not yet learned to perform. The child laughs when something is funny, cries when something hurts, and says what he thinks without editing. The father watches his son and sees — with painful clarity — who he himself used to be.

This poem was written in the post-colonial world of Nigeria, a society that was rapidly urbanising, modernising, and adopting the social codes of the West — codes that placed enormous value on social performance, on the right handshake, the right smile, the right face for the right occasion. Okara looked at this world and felt something being lost. Something warm, something honest, something African. And he missed it the way you miss a home you can no longer return to.

The poem opens with the phrase "Once upon a time" — and notice that immediately. This is a phrase from fairy tales, from bedtime stories, from a world of innocence and imagination. By beginning with these words, Okara is telling us right away that what follows is something that once existed but no longer does. It is the language of memory. It is the language of loss.

Think about this

The title and opening phrase carry the entire emotional weight of the poem before you read a word of the body. "Once upon a time" signals: this is a story about something that is gone. Keep that feeling as you read every stanza.

Stanza 1 — The Death of Genuine Laughter
Once upon a time, son They used to laugh with their hearts And laugh with their eyes: But now they only laugh with their teeth While their ice-block-cold eyes Search behind my shadow.

What the poet is saying: The father begins by speaking to his son about how people used to laugh — genuinely, from the heart, with warmth visible in their eyes. "Laugh with their hearts" means their joy was real and internal, not performed. "Laugh with their eyes" means the emotion reached even their gaze — the eyes lit up along with the face. But now? Now they "only laugh with their teeth." The teeth are bared — the mouth moves into a smile — but the eyes are cold. The eyes are like ice blocks. And those cold eyes are not looking at you with warmth; they are searching behind you, calculating, looking for what they can gain from you.

Notice this

"Search behind my shadow" is one of the most powerful images in the poem. Your shadow falls behind you. The people who appear to smile at your face are actually looking at what is behind you — your wealth, your power, your connections. The smile is a mask. The eyes reveal the truth.

A real-life comparison: Think of the difference between a friend who genuinely laughs at your jokes and a person who laughs at their boss's terrible jokes at a work dinner. Same physical action. Completely different reality.

Figure of speech: "Ice-block-cold eyes" — this is a metaphor. The eyes are compared to ice blocks, suggesting not just coldness but a complete absence of warmth or feeling. It is also an example of Okara's characteristically African imagery — vivid, physical, rooted in the senses.

The poet is really saying...

Genuine human connection is disappearing, replaced by calculated social performance designed to serve self-interest.

Stanza 2 — The Death of Genuine Friendship
There was a time indeed They used to shake hands with their hearts But that's gone, son Now they shake hands without hearts While their left hands search My empty pockets.

What the poet is saying: The handshake, in many cultures, is a symbol of trust, agreement, and warmth. "Shake hands with their hearts" means the gesture was once genuine — you took someone's hand because you genuinely welcomed them, genuinely meant what you said. But now, the right hand extends in apparent friendship while the left hand — secretly, quietly — reaches into your pocket to see what you have. This is the classic image of the two-faced person: the warm public gesture masking a private calculation of what you are worth.

Notice this

"My empty pockets" — the narrator is not actually wealthy or powerful. The people who search his pockets find nothing. This adds a layer of pathos: even the exploitation is fruitless. He has nothing to offer these hypocrites, yet they go through the performance anyway. The hypocrisy has become automatic, mechanical.

Figure of speech: "Shake hands without hearts" — juxtaposition. The physical action (handshake) is placed against its absent emotional content (without hearts) to highlight the hollow nature of modern social rituals.

The poet is really saying...

Social rituals that once expressed real human warmth have been hollowed out and repurposed as tools of social climbing and exploitation.

Stanza 3 — The Welcome That Is Not Welcome
"Feel at home!", "Come again": They say, and when I come Again and feel At home, once, twice There will be no thrice — For then I find doors shut on me.

What the poet is saying: People say "Feel at home!" — an invitation of warmth, of belonging. They say "Come again!" — please return, you are always welcome here. And the narrator believes them. He visits once — all is well. He visits twice — still fine. But the third time he visits, the door is shut. The welcome was never real. It was a social formula, repeated without any genuine feeling or intention. The exclamation marks in "Feel at home!" and "Come again!" are cruelly ironic — the exclamation of warmth becomes the hollow sound of performance.

Notice this — Exam important

"There will be no thrice" — this is one of the most quotable lines in the poem. It means: the third visit will never happen, because by then the door will be closed. The pattern is visit once (tolerated), visit twice (barely tolerated), visit three times — you have overstayed your social welcome. The hospitality was never real; it had an invisible expiry date.

Figure of speech: The use of direct speech ("Feel at home!", "Come again") creates a devastating irony. The words of welcome are rendered meaningless by the action that follows — the shut door. This is called dramatic irony — the gap between what is said and what is true.

Stanza 4 — The Narrator Confesses: He Has Learned to Perform
And I have learned too To laugh with only my teeth And shake hands without my heart I have also learned to say "Goodbye" When I mean "Good-riddance": To say "Glad to meet you" Without being glad; and to say "It's been Nice talking to you", after being bored.

What the poet is saying: Now comes the confession that breaks the poem wide open. The father admits that he — the one telling this story, the one condemning this behaviour — has himself learned it. He too laughs with only his teeth. He too shakes hands without his heart. He says "Goodbye" when he feels relieved to be rid of the person. He says "Glad to meet you" when he felt nothing of the kind. He says "It's been nice talking to you" after a conversation that bored him entirely. He has become what he is criticising.

Notice this — most important stanza

This confession is the emotional heart of the entire poem. The father is not speaking to his son from a position of moral superiority. He is confessing his own corruption. This makes the poem not a lecture about other people's hypocrisy — it is a lament about what the world does to all of us if we live in it long enough. This honesty is what makes Okara a great poet.

Figure of speech: "Say 'Goodbye' when I mean 'Good-riddance'" — antithesis. The social pleasantry is placed directly against the private feeling, revealing the total gap between public performance and private truth.

The poet is really saying...

Even a man who recognises and hates hypocrisy is not immune to it. The social world is so powerful that it gradually reshapes even the most honest person.

Stanza 5 — The Many Faces
So I have learnt many things, son I have learned to wear many faces Like dresses – home face, Office face, street face, host face, Cocktail face, with all their conforming smiles Like a fixed portrait smile.

What the poet is saying: The father now catalogues what he has learned. He wears faces like he wears clothes — changing them depending on where he is and who is watching. There is a "home face" for his family, an "office face" for his colleagues, a "street face" for strangers, a "host face" for guests in his home, a "cocktail face" for social gatherings. Each face comes with its own "conforming smile" — a smile that conforms to what is expected in that setting. And all these smiles, he says, are like a "fixed portrait smile" — the frozen, unchanging smile in a painted portrait that is not alive, not real, not responding to anything real.

Notice this — Exam important

"Wear many faces like dresses" — this simile is one of the most exam-important lines. It will appear in reference-to-context questions. The comparison of faces to dresses suggests that identity itself has become something that can be changed, put on and removed based on social convenience. This is the total corruption of authenticity.

Figure of speech: "Like a fixed portrait smile" — simile. The face in a portrait is painted — it never changes, it shows nothing real, it is performance frozen in time. All these modern social smiles are, in the poet's view, exactly that: frozen, painted, dead.

The poet is really saying...

Modern social life demands that we fragment ourselves into different performances for different audiences, until there is no single true face left — only costumes.

Stanza 6 — The Longing to Return
But believe me, son I want to be what I used to be When I was like you. I want To unlearn all these muting things. Most of all, I want to relearn How to laugh, for my laugh in the mirror Shows only my teeth like a snake's bare fangs!

What the poet is saying: And now the emotional turn. After all this confession, the father says: "But believe me, son." Believe me — because he knows how he sounds, knows he sounds like a hypocrite, knows his son might not trust him. He genuinely, desperately wants to go back. He wants to unlearn what he has learned. He calls it "muting things" — these social performances that mute his true self, silence his genuine voice. Most of all, he wants to relearn how to laugh. And then the devastating image: when he laughs in the mirror, all he sees is his teeth — like a snake's bare fangs. Not warmth. Not joy. Just bared teeth. The same hollow performance that he condemned in others. He has become what he feared.

Notice this — Exam important

"Like a snake's bare fangs" — this simile is the climax of the poem. The snake bares its fangs as a threat, as a weapon. The narrator's smile, similarly, is not an expression of joy — it is bared teeth with no warmth behind them. He sees himself as something predatory, something dangerous, something that has lost its humanity. This is the moment of maximum self-awareness and self-horror.

Stanza 7 — The Plea: Teach Me
So show me, son, How to laugh; show me how I used to laugh and smile Once upon a time when I was like you.

What the poet is saying: The final stanza is the most beautiful and the most unexpected. The father — the adult, the experienced person, the one who is supposed to teach — turns to his child and asks to be taught. Show me, son. Show me how to laugh the way I used to laugh. The phrase "once upon a time" returns, completing the circle. The title was the beginning of a fairy tale — and the ending brings us back to it, to a time of innocence that existed once and that the father longs to find again through his child.

The poem as a whole

At its deepest level, "Once Upon a Time" is a poem about the cost of growing up in a society built on performance rather than truth. Okara does not blame any individual — he blames the world that shaped him. The child in the poem is not just a child; he is a symbol of who all of us used to be before we learned to wear faces. The father's plea — "show me how to laugh" — is not just personal. It is the plea of every person who has felt themselves becoming someone they did not intend to become. The poem ends not with despair but with hope: the child is still here. The innocence is not gone from the world — it is still alive, right there in his son. And perhaps, if he watches carefully, he can find his way back.

They used to laugh with their hearts / And laugh with their eyes

What it means: In earlier times, laughter was genuine — it came from the heart (real emotion) and reached the eyes (visible sincerity). Both body and soul participated in the expression of joy.

Why it matters for the exam

Appears in Reference to Context, comparison questions ("contrast past and present"), and theme questions about loss of authenticity. This line establishes the benchmark of genuine emotion against which all the hypocrisy is measured.

Figure of speech: Repetition / anaphora — "laugh with their hearts / laugh with their eyes" — the repeated structure emphasises the wholeness of genuine emotion.

Key word: "Hearts" — the heart represents authentic feeling. The shift from laughing with the heart to laughing with only teeth marks the central movement of the poem.

But now they only laugh with their teeth / While their ice-block-cold eyes / Search behind my shadow.

What it means: Modern social laughter is merely physical — the mouth smiles but the eyes remain cold and calculating, scanning for what they can gain.

Why it matters for the exam

This is the single most important extract — expect it in Reference to Context with questions on figure of speech and meaning. "Ice-block-cold eyes" and "search behind my shadow" are the two phrases examiners most commonly ask about.

Figure of speech: Metaphor — "ice-block-cold eyes" compares the eyes to ice blocks, denoting complete emotional absence.

Key word: "Search" — the active verb reveals that this is not passive observation; these people are actively investigating, calculating, pursuing self-interest even while smiling.

There will be no thrice —

What it means: The welcome extended by modern society has an invisible expiry. Visit once — tolerated. Visit twice — still acceptable. Visit a third time — the door is shut. The hospitality was always false.

Why it matters for the exam

Short, sharp, and memorable — this line appears in one-sentence answer questions and reference-to-context. Examiners ask: "What does 'there will be no thrice' mean?" Answer: the person is unwelcome on the third visit because the pretended friendship has a limit.

Figure of speech: Irony — the warm invitation ("Feel at home!") contrasted with the shut door on the third visit.

Key word: "Thrice" — an unusual, archaic word that echoes the "once upon a time" fairy-tale register, deepening the contrast between the world of genuine warmth (fairy tales) and harsh reality.

I have learned to wear many faces / Like dresses

What it means: The narrator has learned to change his personality, his manner, and his expression the way one changes clothes — depending on the social occasion.

Why it matters for the exam

This simile is central to character questions ("How does the narrator describe himself?") and theme questions ("How does the poem show fragmentation of identity?"). The word "dresses" carries deep irony — clothing protects and presents us; the narrator uses faces the same way.

Figure of speech: Simile — "like dresses" directly compares faces to clothing, implying that identity is costume.

Key word: "Wear" — you wear something external. It is not part of you. The face is not the self.

My laugh in the mirror / Shows only my teeth like a snake's bare fangs!

What it means: When the narrator looks at his own reflection, he does not see joy — he sees bared teeth, like a snake preparing to strike. His smile has become a threat display, not an expression of happiness.

Why it matters for the exam

This is the climax of the poem and appears in all question types. For figure of speech: "like a snake's bare fangs" is a simile. For theme: it shows how completely the narrator has been transformed by the hypocritical world around him. This line will appear in the exam.

Figure of speech: Simile — "like a snake's bare fangs" compares the narrator's smile to a snake's fangs: dangerous, cold, predatory rather than warm.

Key word: "Mirror" — the mirror forces honesty. He cannot perform for himself. The mirror is the one audience before which all social masks fail.

I want to unlearn all these muting things.

What it means: The narrator desperately wants to reverse the process — to shed everything he has learned that silences ("mutes") his true self and genuine emotions.

Why it matters for the exam

The word "unlearn" is the thematic crux — it appears in fill-in-the-blank questions and vocabulary questions. "Muting things" — what are they? The social performances that silence authentic emotion.

Figure of speech: Metaphor — calling social habits "muting things" compares them to a sound suppressor — something that silences the true voice.

Key word: "Muting" — to mute is to silence. These social habits have silenced the narrator's genuine self.

Themes
Hypocrisy and Social Performance

The central theme of the poem is the triumph of performance over genuine feeling in modern society. Okara illustrates this through a series of concrete images: the laugh that uses only teeth, the handshake without a heart, the welcome that vanishes on the third visit. Each image shows a social ritual — laughing, shaking hands, welcoming guests — that has been drained of its authentic content and refilled with calculation. In a paragraph or essay on this theme, a student should argue: Okara presents hypocrisy not as a personal moral failing but as a systemic condition — the natural result of a society that rewards performance and punishes sincerity. The evidence is in the shift from "they" to "I": even the narrator, who sees the problem clearly, has been infected by it. This universality is what makes the poem so powerful.

Lost Innocence and the Longing to Return

The second great theme is the loss of childhood innocence and the adult's desperate longing to recover it. The poem begins and ends with "once upon a time" — the language of fairy tales and childhood. Between those two occurrences, the narrator charts his own fall from innocent authenticity into performed hypocrisy. The child in the poem has not yet learned to perform — he still laughs with his whole face, still means what he says. For the narrator, the child is not just his son; he is a mirror of his former self, the self that existed "once upon a time." In an essay on this theme, argue: The poem traces the inevitable price of social maturity — the gradual suppression of genuine feeling in exchange for social acceptance. The narrator's plea at the end ("show me how to laugh") is not weakness; it is a profound recognition that what was lost was something essential to being human.

Modern Society and the Death of Authentic Human Connection

Okara writes this poem as a critique of the post-colonial African society he inhabits — but the critique extends to any modern, urbanised, materially competitive society. The modern world, as Okara sees it, transforms every human interaction into a transaction. A smile becomes a tool. A handshake becomes a probe for wealth. A welcome becomes a calculated social courtesy. "Face is the index of the mind" — the old adage suggests that what shows on the face reflects what is in the mind. Okara's poem argues the opposite: in the modern world, the face has been deliberately disconnected from the mind, trained to show what is socially useful rather than what is emotionally true. In an essay on this theme, argue: The poem is "nothing but a criticism of modern life" (the textbook's own phrase) because it shows, systematically, how every form of basic human warmth — laughter, greeting, hospitality, farewell — has been corrupted by self-interest.

Literary Devices
Metaphor

"Ice-block-cold eyes" — the eyes are compared to blocks of ice, suggesting complete emotional coldness and the absence of any warmth or feeling. This device is effective because it gives the abstract quality (emotional coldness) a physical, sensory reality — we can almost feel the coldness of an ice block.

Exam tip

Name the device, quote the exact phrase, and explain what is being compared and why it is effective. For "ice-block-cold eyes": the eyes are compared to ice blocks; this is effective because ice blocks are not just cold — they are frozen, inert, and incapable of warmth.

Simile

"I have learned to wear many faces / Like dresses" — the narrator's changing social personas are compared to clothing. This is a simile because of the word "like." It is devastatingly effective because it implies that identity — who we are — has become as easily changeable and as externally motivated as what we wear.

Second simile: "My laugh in the mirror / Shows only my teeth like a snake's bare fangs" — the narrator's hollow smile is compared to a snake displaying its fangs. The comparison to fangs suggests that the smile has become predatory and dangerous rather than warm and genuine.

Repetition / Anaphora

"Once upon a time" — repeated at the opening of the poem (as title and first line) and again at the final line. This structural repetition creates a circularity — the poem ends where it began, suggesting that the narrator is trapped in his longing, unable to actually return to the state he desires.

"I have learned... I have also learned... I have learnt" — the repetition of "I have learned" in stanza 4 creates a devastating accumulation effect, listing all the things the narrator has been trained to do against his own authentic feelings.

Irony

The poem is deeply ironic throughout. The greatest irony is that the narrator — who tells his son about the hypocrisy of others — admits that he has become equally hypocritical. He cannot stand outside the system he critiques. Another irony: "Feel at home!" followed by "doors shut on me" — the language of welcome produces the experience of exclusion.

Imagery

The poem is rich in concrete, physical images: ice-block-cold eyes, empty pockets, shut doors, the mirror, the snake's fangs. Okara was committed to rooting abstract ideas in physical sensation — this is the African oral tradition influence in his work. The imagery makes the emotional reality of the poem impossible to dismiss.

Section A — Answer in one or two sentences
Question a

What do you associate with the title of the poem?

The phrase "Once upon a time" is typically associated with fairy tales and children's stories, evoking a world of innocence, imagination, and happy beginnings. In this poem, Okara uses it to signal that genuine warmth and honest human relationships belong to a lost past, now accessible only through memory.
Question b

What is the relationship between the narrator and the listener?

The narrator is a father, and the listener is his young son. The father speaks to his child in an intimate, confessional tone, lamenting the hypocrisy he has witnessed and adopted in adult society, and finally asking his son to teach him how to laugh genuinely as he once did.
Question c

What happens to the poet when he visits someone for the third time?

When the poet visits someone for the third time, he finds the door shut on him. Despite having been warmly invited with "Feel at home!" and "Come again!", the welcome was never genuine, and by the third visit the pretended hospitality has expired, revealing the hollowness of the social pleasantries.
Question d

Pick out the expressions that indicate conflicting ideas.

The expressions that indicate conflicting ideas are: "Feel at home!" versus "doors shut on me"; "Glad to meet you" versus "without being glad"; "It's been nice talking to you" versus "after being bored"; and "Goodbye" versus "Good-riddance." Each pair shows the gulf between what is said and what is actually felt.
Question e

How does the poet compare his face with dresses?

The poet says he has learned to wear many faces "like dresses" — just as one changes clothes to suit different occasions (home, office, street, party), he changes his facial expression and social manner to suit different social contexts, including a home face, office face, street face, host face, and cocktail face.
Question f

What does the poet mean when he says 'goodbye'?

When the poet says "Goodbye," he actually means "Good-riddance" — he is inwardly relieved to be free of the person's company but socially performs the pleasantry of a warm farewell. This reveals the total disconnection between his private feelings and his public behaviour.
Question g

What pleasantries does the poet use to fake cordiality?

The poet fakes cordiality by saying "Feel at home!", "Come again", "Glad to meet you", and "It's been nice talking to you" — all standard social expressions of warmth — while privately feeling indifference, boredom, or relief at the person's departure.
Question h

What does he desire to unlearn and relearn?

The narrator desires to unlearn "all these muting things" — the social performances, fake smiles, hollow handshakes, and insincere pleasantries that silence his genuine self. Most of all, he wants to relearn how to laugh genuinely, with his whole heart and face, the way he did when he was a child like his son.
Question i

How is the poet's laugh reflected in the mirror?

When the poet laughs before the mirror, he sees only his teeth — bared like a snake's fangs. There is no warmth, no genuine joy in the expression; only the physical action of baring teeth, which is indistinguishable from a threat display rather than a smile of happiness.
Question j

What does the poet long for?

The poet longs to return to the innocent, genuine self he was when he was a child — to laugh with his heart and his eyes, to shake hands with sincerity, and to mean every word he says. He asks his son to show him how to laugh as he once did, "once upon a time."
Question k

Mention the qualities the child in the poem symbolises.

The child in the poem symbolises innocence, authenticity, and genuine human feeling. He represents the ability to laugh with the heart, to mean what one says, and to engage with the world without social calculation or performance. He is what the narrator once was and longs to become again.
Section C — Answer in 100–150 words
Question i

Explain the things the poet has learnt when he grew into an adult.

As the narrator grew into an adult, he learned a series of social performances that replaced his earlier genuineness. He learned to laugh with only his teeth — to smile externally without any corresponding internal feeling. He learned to shake hands without his heart — to go through the motions of friendship without genuine warmth. He learned to say "Goodbye" when he meant "Good-riddance," to say "Glad to meet you" without actually feeling glad, and to claim a conversation was pleasant after finding it utterly boring. He learned to wear many different faces, like different outfits, depending on the social context — a home face, an office face, a street face, a host face, and a cocktail face, each one carrying its own conforming, artificial smile. All of these things, he tells his son with regret, are what adulthood in the modern world taught him — and all of them together have silenced the genuine person he once was.
Question ii

This poem is nothing but a criticism of modern life. Justify this statement.

Gabriel Okara's "Once Upon a Time" is a sustained and systematic criticism of modern life, targeting each of its central social rituals in turn. Laughter, once genuine, has become a performance — teeth bared while cold eyes calculate what can be gained. Friendship, once warm, has become a probe — hands extended in greeting while the other hand searches empty pockets. Hospitality, once heartfelt, has become a social formula with an invisible expiry date — "Come again!" said with no intention of following through. Even the narrator himself has been transformed by this world: he now wears multiple faces like costume changes, performs pleasantries he does not feel, and finds that his own smile in the mirror resembles not joy but a snake's fangs. Okara presents modern society as a machine that takes honest human beings and trains them, systematically and inevitably, into performers and hypocrites. The poem is a criticism not of any individual but of the entire social environment — an indictment of the world adults are compelled to inhabit.

Literary Interpretation — Explain each expression

3.A. Interpret each of the following expressions in one or two lines:

i. laugh with their eyes: To laugh with the eyes means to laugh genuinely — when real joy is felt, it reaches even the eyes, which light up and crinkle with authentic emotion.

ii. shake hands without hearts: To shake hands without the heart means to go through the physical gesture of greeting without any genuine warmth or sincerity — a purely mechanical social ritual.

iii. like a fixed portrait smile: A portrait smile is frozen in time — it is painted, unchanging, and responds to nothing. Modern social smiles are like this: artificial, uniform, and utterly detached from real feeling.

iv. hands search my empty pockets: While appearing to offer friendly greetings, people secretly assess the narrator's wealth and social status — but find nothing of value, as his pockets are empty.

v. to unlearn all these muting things: "Muting things" refers to all the social performances that the narrator has learned as an adult — habits that silence ("mute") his genuine self and authentic emotions. He desperately wants to shed them.
Reference to Context — 3.B

i. "But now they only laugh with their teeth, / While their ice-block-cold eyes…"

a) Who are 'they'? "They" refers to the people in modern society — acquaintances, colleagues, and social contacts who perform warmth without feeling it.

b) Explain: ice-block-cold eyes: "Ice-block-cold eyes" is a metaphor comparing the eyes to blocks of ice — they are completely without warmth, frozen, and devoid of genuine emotion. The eyes reveal the truth that the smiling mouth conceals.

c) Figure of speech: Metaphor — "ice-block-cold eyes" directly compares the eyes to ice blocks without using "like" or "as."
Reference to Context — 3.B ii

"Most of all, I want to relearn / How to laugh, for my laugh in the mirror / Shows only my teeth like a snake's bare fangs!"

a) Why does the poet want to relearn how to laugh? The narrator wants to relearn how to laugh because his current laugh — when he sees it in the mirror — shows only bared teeth like a snake's fangs, with no genuine warmth or joy. His laughter has become as hollow as the performances he once criticised in others.

b) Whom does the poet want to relearn from? He wants to relearn from his young son, who still laughs genuinely and represents the innocence the narrator has lost.

c) Figure of speech: Simile — "like a snake's bare fangs" directly compares the narrator's bared-teeth smile to the fangs of a snake using the word "like."

Parallel Reading — Rock Me to Sleep

The poem "Rock Me to Sleep" by Elizabeth Akers Allen shares the theme of longing for an innocent past. Read the poem and compare it with "Once Upon a Time."

Both "Once Upon a Time" and "Rock Me to Sleep" express a profound longing to return to an earlier state of innocence and genuine connection. Okara's narrator longs for his childhood self — a time of authentic laughter and sincere relationships. Allen's speaker longs for her mother's love and the safety of childhood. Both poems use the motif of time reversing ("Once upon a time" / "Backward, turn backward, O Time"). Both recognise that adulthood brings weariness, compromise, and loss of what was most real. The key difference is perspective: Okara focuses on social hypocrisy as the cause of loss; Allen focuses on grief and the physical absence of a beloved mother.
