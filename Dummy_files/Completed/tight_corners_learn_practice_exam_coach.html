About the Author

Let us meet Edward Verrall Lucas, who lived from 1868 to 1938. The textbook introduces him as an English humorist, essayist, playwright, biographer, publisher, poet, novelist, short story writer, and editor. This wide range is important because “Tight Corners” shows a mind trained to observe people, situations, and social embarrassment with comic accuracy.

Lucas was born to a Quaker family on the fringes of London. He began work at the age of sixteen, apprenticed to a bookseller. After that, he turned to journalism and worked on a local paper in Brighton and then on a London evening paper. He was commissioned to write a biography of Bernard Barton, the Quaker poet, and this led to further literary work, including the editing of Charles Lamb’s works.

In 1904, Lucas joined the staff of the humorous magazine Punch and remained there for the rest of his life. The textbook says he was a prolific writer, most celebrated for his short essays, though he also produced verses, novels, and plays. That means humour, short prose, and quick social observation were at the centre of his talent.

Why this matters for this text: In “Tight Corners,” Lucas turns a small social mistake into a thrilling mental crisis. Nothing physically dangerous happens to the narrator, yet the panic feels real. Knowing Lucas as a humorist helps us understand the lesson: rash playfulness can create misery, but wit and presence of mind can save a person at the last moment.

Introduction / Before We Read

Have you ever done something “just for fun” and then suddenly realized it could become serious? That is the emotional trap in this lesson. The narrator attends an auction, raises bids casually, and then discovers that one careless addition of fifty guineas has made him the buyer of a painting worth more than he can possibly pay.

The title “Tight Corners” means difficult situations. The warm-up explains how auctions work: buyers enter a sale-room, decide what they wish to buy, get a registration card, and bid higher prices until the highest bidder wins. This process is exciting only if the bidder has money and intention. In this lesson, the narrator has neither.

The talk was running on the critical situations in which we had found ourselves — those of us whose lives were adventurous enough to comprise any.

The opening is conversational. A group of people discuss dangerous situations, but one man says that the tightest corners can be mental, not physical. That prepares us for the auction-room crisis.

Think about this

The narrator is not chased by a tiger or trapped by a tide. He is trapped by his own voice saying “And fifty!” That is why the lesson is funny and painful at the same time.

Themes and Literary Devices
Theme

Rash playfulness can become a serious crisis. The narrator enters Christie’s only because his friend persuades him, and he begins bidding “just for fun.” As long as others bid higher, he is safe. But when he adds “And fifty!” to the dealer’s four-thousand-guinea bid, the hammer falls and he becomes the buyer. In an essay answer, write that Lucas warns students not to treat serious public situations as games.

Theme

A mental tight corner can be worse than a physical danger. At the beginning, people speak of tides, tigers, burning houses, and war. The narrator replies that mental tight corners can be tighter. His panic is internal: blood congeals, he thinks of borrowing, moneylenders, confession, and even a firing party. This makes the lesson a study of psychological crisis.

Theme

Presence of mind can rescue a person when luck appears. The narrator’s luck begins when the dealer offers fifty guineas for his bid. But he does not accept immediately. He asks if that is the most the dealer would offer and finally demands a hundred. In a paragraph answer, write that luck saves him, but quick thinking turns the rescue into profit.

Humour

“I had exactly sixty-three pounds in the bank... and here I was nodding away to the auctioneer like a bloatocrat.” The comedy comes from the contrast between his poverty and his rich-man behaviour.

Hyperbole

“My heart stopped; my blood congealed.” The narrator exaggerates his terror to make the mental crisis feel dramatic and funny.

Irony

“A career of rectitude sometimes has rewards beyond the mere consciousness of virtue.” He was in danger because of foolishness, yet he presents his rescue as if virtue itself rewarded him.
