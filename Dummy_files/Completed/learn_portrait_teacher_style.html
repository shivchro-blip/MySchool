Before we read any story, we always ask — who wrote this, and why should we care? So let me introduce you to the man behind this beautiful piece.

Khushwant Singh was born in 1915 in Hadali — a village that is now in Pakistan. He lived a long, extraordinary life and passed away in 2014 at the age of 99. Yes — 99 years old. And he was writing almost till the very end. That itself tells you something about the man.

The educated man
Education
St. Stephen's College, Delhi — one of India's finest colleges, known for producing brilliant minds. Then King's College, London — a world-renowned university in England. One foot in India, one foot in the West. He understood both worlds deeply.
Career
Trained lawyer · Joined the Indian Foreign Service in 1947, the very year India became independent · Eventually left diplomacy for full-time writing and editing · Edited some of India's most important literary magazines and two major newspapers.
Awards
Padma Bhushan (1974) · Padma Vibhushan from the Government of India · Sahitya Akademi Fellowship — the highest honour given to a writer by India's national academy of letters.
Notable works
The Train to Pakistan · The Mark of Vishnu · A History of Sikhs · We Indians · Death at my Doorstep · Success Mantra
What made his writing special

Khushwant Singh had three very distinctive qualities as a writer. First — keen secularism. He believed deeply that all religions deserved equal respect. He was a Sikh who could write beautifully about Hindu grandmothers and Muslim neighbours with equal warmth.

Second — sarcasm. He had a razor-sharp wit. He could be funny and cutting at the same time. He never hesitated to call out hypocrisy — in politicians, in society, in religion.

Third — love for poetry. Despite being known as a prose writer, he had a deep love for poetry — both Urdu and English. This love for the musical quality of language shows in his prose too. Read The Portrait of a Lady carefully and you will feel the rhythm in his sentences.

Why this matters for the story
Khushwant Singh grew up partly in a village, then moved to Delhi, then studied in London, then worked abroad. His life followed almost the same arc as the grandson in the story — from a rooted, traditional life to a modern, educated, cosmopolitan one. So when he writes about the grandmother being left behind as the grandson moves forward — he is writing about himself. About his own guilt, his own nostalgia, his own love for a world he moved away from. That is what makes the story so honest. So human.
Before we read — let's think

Close your eyes for a second. Think about your grandmother. How does she look? What does she smell like? What does she always do — maybe she is always in the kitchen, or always praying, or always watching her favourite serial.

Now imagine writing about her — not just how she looks, but everything she meant to you.

That is exactly what Khushwant Singh did. And the result is one of the most beautiful pieces of writing in Indian English literature.

What kind of writing is this?

This story is autobiographical — meaning it actually happened to him. He is writing about his own grandmother. That is why every small detail feels so real — the wooden slate, the yellow chalk, the stale chapattis. He did not make this up. He lived it.

The full title is "The Portrait of a Lady" — not a painted portrait, but a word portrait. Singh is painting his grandmother using words instead of colours. And by the end, you will feel like you know her personally.

The grandmother — how Singh describes her

Singh opens the story with a striking admission — he says his grandmother had been old and wrinkled for the twenty years that he had known her. People said she had once been young and pretty, but to him — that seemed almost revolting. He simply could not imagine her as anything other than old.

She was short, fat, slightly bent. Her face was a "criss-cross of wrinkles running from everywhere to everywhere." Her silver hair was scattered untidily over her pale, puckered face. Her lips constantly moved in inaudible prayer.

And then — he says something unforgettable.

"She could never have been pretty; but she was always beautiful."
Read that again slowly. Pretty and beautiful are not the same thing. Pretty is about your face. Beautiful is about who you are. Despite her wrinkles, her bent posture, her scattered hair — she was profoundly beautiful. Because beauty, Singh is telling us, comes from within. From purity, from devotion, from love.
"She was like the winter landscape in the mountains, an expanse of pure white serenity breathing peace and contentment."
This is a Simile — she is compared to a snowy mountain landscape using the word "like." Think about what a snowy mountain is — vast, open, white, utterly still and peaceful. That is exactly what she radiated. Not just a little bit of calm, but an overwhelming, all-encompassing serenity. Spend time on this line in class — it captures everything about her in one breath.

She always wore spotless white — one hand resting on her waist to balance her stoop, the other telling the beads of her rosary. She hobbled about the house. She was always praying — silently, continuously, her lips moving even when no sound came out.

This is your first picture of her. Hold it in your mind as we follow her through three phases of her life.

Phase 1 — The Village
The golden period. Their bond is at its strongest here. They are inseparable — from morning to night, every single day.
The morning routine

Picture this morning routine in the village — and notice how every tiny detail Singh gives us tells us something about the grandmother's love.

She wakes him up. She says her morning prayer in a monotonous sing-song while she bathes and dresses him — hoping he will listen and learn it by heart. He listens because he loves her voice — but never bothers to learn the prayer. Already, even as a child, the distance is beginning. But she keeps hoping anyway.

Then she fetches his wooden slate — which she has already washed and plastered with yellow chalk. She ties the inkpot and red pen in a bundle and hands it to him. Then breakfast — a thick, stale chapatti with a little butter and sugar. And then they walk to school together.

She also carries several stale chapattis with her. Not for him. For the village dogs.

Notice this
She prepared his slate before he was even awake. She thought of the dogs before leaving the house. Every morning, before she had done anything for herself, she had already thought of everyone else. That is the nature of her love — it is always ahead of itself, always giving before being asked.
The village school

The school was attached to the temple — and this is important. It means learning and religion existed in the same space. The priest taught the alphabet and the morning prayer. The children sat in rows on either side of the verandah, singing the alphabet and prayer in chorus.

And grandmother? She sat inside — reading the scriptures. They were in different rooms, doing different things — but they were together. That togetherness is the essence of this phase.

When school finished, they walked back together. And this time, the village dogs were waiting at the temple door. They followed the grandmother and grandson home — "growling and fighting with each other for the chapatti we threw to them." Noisy, chaotic, joyful. The dogs knew she was coming. They always knew.

The village school vs the city school — a key difference

The village school taught the alphabet and prayer together — God and learning were not separate. Grandmother could participate, could sit nearby, could feel connected to what he was learning.

In the city, the English school would teach gravity, Archimedes, science — things she neither understood nor believed in. And it would teach no prayer at all. That difference — between the village school and the city school — is the difference between the two worlds pulling at this story.

Phase 2 — The City
The turning point. Life moves forward — and quietly, without drama, pulls the grandson away. The bond does not break all at once. It frays, slowly, thread by thread.
The turning point

When the parents are comfortably settled in the city, they send for the grandmother and grandson. Singh calls this — "a turning point in our friendship."

Notice the word "friendship." He does not call it their relationship or their bond. He calls it a friendship. That is intentional. Their love was not just the love between a grandchild and grandparent — it was a genuine friendship. Two people who liked each other's company. And it was that friendship that the city would begin to erode.

Although they shared the same room, grandmother no longer came to school with him. He took a motor bus to an English school. There were no dogs in the city streets. And so she began feeding sparrows in the courtyard.

The sparrows — a crucial detail
In the village, she fed the dogs — noisy, rough, chaotic dogs. In the city, she feeds sparrows — small, quiet, gentle birds. Even her companions have become smaller and quieter. Her world has shrunk. But she has not broken. She simply finds something new to love.
The slow drift

For some time she continued to wake him up and get him ready for school. When he came back, she would ask what the teacher had taught. He would tell her — English words, the law of gravity, Archimedes' Principle, the world being round. This made her unhappy.

She could not help him with his lessons. She did not believe in the things they taught at the English school. And she was distressed — there was no teaching about God, no scriptures, no prayer.

Then one day, he announces — "We are being given music lessons."

She says nothing.

But her silence means everything.

To her, music was associated with dancing girls and disreputable people — it had no place in a school, no place in respectable education. She rarely talked to him much after that.

"The common link of friendship was snapped."
This happens when he goes to university and gets his own room. Notice the word "snapped" — not loosened, not weakened. Snapped — like a twig, or a rubber band pulled too tight. The break is sudden and complete, even though nothing dramatic happened. Life simply moved forward. And that was enough.
Her life after the snap

She accepted her seclusion with resignation — without protest, without anger. From sunrise to sunset she sat at her spinning wheel, spinning and reciting prayers. Only in the afternoon did she relax — to feed the sparrows.

Picture her in the verandah — breaking bread into little bits. Hundreds of little birds collecting around her, creating a veritable bedlam of chirruping. Some perched on her legs, others on her shoulders. Some even sat on her head. She smiled but never shooed them away. That half-hour was the happiest time of her day.

The grandson had university. She had sparrows. And she was content.

Phase 3 — The Farewell and Return
Love that asks for nothing. The final chapter — and the most beautiful one.
Going abroad

When he decides to go abroad for further studies, he is sure his grandmother will be upset. He will be away for five years. At her age, one could never tell.

But his grandmother surprises him completely.

She was not even sentimental. She came to the railway station to see him off — but did not talk, did not show any emotion. Her lips moved in prayer. Her mind was lost in prayer. Her fingers were busy telling the beads of her rosary.

And then — silently — she kissed his forehead.

"Silently she kissed my forehead, and when I left I cherished the moist imprint as perhaps the last sign of physical contact between us."
That moist kiss on the forehead. He holds onto it. He calls it "perhaps the last sign of physical contact between us" — because he genuinely did not know if she would be alive when he returned. He was saying goodbye to someone who might be gone. And she gave him a silent kiss and let him go. That is the depth of her love — she did not make it about herself. Not even once.
The return

Five years pass. He comes back. She is at the station. She does not look a day older. She still has no time for words — and while she clasps him in her arms, he can hear her still reciting her prayers. Even in that embrace, she is praying.

That evening — something completely unexpected happens.

She stops praying.

She gathers all the women of the neighbourhood. She finds an old, broken drum. And she begins to sing — songs of warriors coming home. For several hours she thumps the sagging skins of that dilapidated drum and sings. They have to persuade her to stop before she strains herself.

Why is this so significant?
In the entire story — this is the only time she skips her prayers. For her, prayer is as natural as breathing. It is not a habit, it is who she is. But that evening, her joy overflows so completely that even prayer cannot contain it. Her grandson has come home. And that — for one single evening — is bigger than everything else.
The final morning

The next morning she falls ill. Mild fever. The doctor says it will pass.

But grandmother knows differently.

She tells the family — her end is near. Since she had omitted to pray just a few hours before the close of her life, she was not going to waste any more time talking. She ignores their protests. She lies peacefully in bed — telling her beads, lips moving in prayer.

And then — quietly, peacefully —

"Her lips stopped moving and the rosary fell from her lifeless fingers."
That is it. Death described in one perfect line. No long death scene, no screaming, no melodrama. Just — the rosary falls. And we know. This restraint is what makes it so powerful. Singh trusts the image to carry everything. He is right.
The sparrows' goodbye

When the family comes in the evening to carry her body, they stop halfway in the courtyard.

Because all over the verandah — all over her room — right up to where she lies dead and still, wrapped in a red shroud — thousands of sparrows sit in complete silence. No chirruping. Not a sound.

The mother fetches some bread and breaks it into little crumbs — the way grandmother used to — and throws it to the sparrows.

The sparrows take no notice of the bread.

When grandmother's body is carried away, they fly off quietly. The next morning, the sweeper sweeps the untouched bread crumbs into the dustbin.

The most haunting moment in the story
The sparrows came not for the food. They never came for the food. They came for her. Their refusal to eat is their silent tribute. Their quiet departure when she is carried away is their farewell. Even the birds understood what the grandson's modern life had made him forget — that she was irreplaceable. Not because of what she gave, but because of who she was.
What is this story really about?

On the surface — it is about a grandmother.

But underneath, it is about how modern life quietly distances us from the people who love us most. The city, the English school, the university, the foreign trip — none of these are evil. They are just life moving forward. But every step forward pulls the grandson a step away from his grandmother.

And she — never once complains. Never once makes him feel guilty. She simply adjusts, prays, and loves. She is the still centre of a spinning world.

The four themes

1. Changing relationships in modern life. Love remains constant — but physical closeness, shared routine, daily connection — these erode. The story charts that erosion with painful honesty.

2. Tradition vs Modernity. Village school vs English school. Scripture vs Archimedes. Music as sacred vs music as entertainment. Two worlds, two value systems — and the grandmother belongs entirely to one, the grandson moves entirely into the other.

3. Selfless love. The grandmother never demands anything in return. Not once. She gives, gives, gives — and when she has nothing left to give, she prays. Her love is the cleanest, most uncomplicated love in the story. Perhaps in all of literature.

4. The innocence of nature. The dogs in the village, the sparrows in the city — they understand something the humans in this story never articulate. The sparrows' silence and their refusal to eat say more about the grandmother's worth than anything any human character says or does.

Why Khushwant Singh wrote this

Remember — Singh himself lived this journey. From a village childhood to London education to life abroad. When he writes about the grandmother being left behind, he is writing about his own guilt. His own nostalgia. His own love for a world he moved away from and could never go back to.

The story is an act of remembrance. And also an act of apology.

These are the six lines every student must know — not just memorise, but truly understand. In the exam, these are the lines that appear in Reference to Context questions.

"She could never have been pretty; but she was always beautiful."
Pretty is about appearance — symmetrical features, clear skin. Beautiful is about character — warmth, purity, devotion, love. Grandmother had none of the first, and all of the second. This single line is Singh's entire argument about what beauty really means.
"She was like the winter landscape in the mountains, an expanse of pure white serenity breathing peace and contentment."
Simile. She is compared to a snow-covered mountain — vast, white, utterly still, radiating peace. The word "expanse" is key — her serenity is not small or modest. It is overwhelming, filling the room, filling the house. Figure of speech: Simile (using "like").
"The common link of friendship was snapped."
"Snapped" — not loosened, not broken gradually. One sudden, clean break. The choice of this word tells us Singh felt the break sharply — even if nothing dramatic caused it. Just a university room of his own was enough. Metaphor — their bond compared to a thread that snaps.
"Silently she kissed my forehead, and when I left I cherished the moist imprint as perhaps the last sign of physical contact between us."
The word "perhaps" carries enormous weight. He knew she might not survive the five years. He was saying goodbye to someone who might be gone. She gave him a silent kiss and let him go without a tear. That is love with no conditions attached.
"Her lips stopped moving and the rosary fell from her lifeless fingers."
Death described without melodrama. One image — the rosary falling — carries the weight of an entire life ending. The restraint here is a deliberate artistic choice. Singh trusts the reader to feel what he refuses to say. It is the finest line in the story.
"The sparrows took no notice of the bread."
Six words. The most haunting sentence in the story. They did not come for the bread. They came for her. Their refusal is their mourning — silent, absolute, and more moving than any human grief in the story.

Every word in the glossary is a potential exam question — synonyms, antonyms, fill in the blanks. Let us go through each word the way a teacher would — not just the meaning, but how to remember it and where it appears in the story.

Mantelpiece
Shelf projecting from the wall above a fireplace — a place of honour in old homes
Used when: Grandfather's portrait hung above the mantelpiece in the drawing room — the most prominent spot in the house
Absurd
Inconsistent or illogical — completely unreasonable
Used when: Singh found grandmother's childhood stories absurd — he could not imagine her ever being young and playful. Antonym: reasonable, logical
Fables
Tales or stories — usually ones that teach a moral lesson
Used when: They treated her childhood stories like the fables of the Prophets — entertaining but not quite believable
Hobbled
Walked unsteadily, with difficulty — the way a very old person moves
Used when: She hobbled about the house in spotless white. Memory tip: when your leg falls asleep and you try to walk, you hobble. Antonym: strode, walked steadily
Puckered
Wrinkled, gathered into folds
Used when: Her silver locks scattered over her pale, puckered face. Think of a drawstring bag pulled tight — the fabric puckers
Expanse
Wide, open, continuous area — like the expanse of a desert or ocean
Used when: "an expanse of pure white serenity" — her peace was not small. It was vast and all-encompassing
Monotonous
Unchanging, repetitive — the same tone over and over
Used when: She said her morning prayer in a monotonous sing-song. Not a criticism — it is simply how prayers are recited. Antonym: interesting, varied
Snapped
Broke suddenly and completely — like a twig or a thread
Used when: "the common link of friendship was snapped" — not slowly worn away but suddenly cut
Seclusion
Isolation — being cut off from others, being alone
Used when: she accepted her seclusion with resignation — her isolation in the city after he went to university. Antonym: companionship, togetherness
Bedlam
Noisy confusion and chaos
Used when: hundreds of birds created a veritable bedlam of chirruping. Originally the name of a famous mental asylum in London — became a word for chaos. Use it in essays: "the classroom erupted into bedlam"
Perched
Sat or rested lightly — the way a bird sits on a branch
Used when: some came and perched on her legs, others on her shoulders. Light, delicate, balanced
Rebukes
Scoldings or reprimands
Used when: she fed them longer and with frivolous rebukes. "Frivolous" means playful — so she was fondly scolding them, the way you might say "you greedy little thing!" to a puppy
Dilapidated
Old, broken down, in a state of disrepair
Used when: she thumped the sagging skins of the dilapidated drum. The drum was damaged and worn — but she played it with full joy anyway
Pallor
Unhealthy pale appearance — the whiteness that comes over a face after death or illness
Used when: a peaceful pallor spread on her face — Singh uses this beautifully; her pallor in death was not frightening. It was peaceful. Antonym: flush, blush
Shroud
Cloth used to wrap a dead body before cremation or burial
Used when: they covered her with a red shroud — a Hindu funeral tradition. Red against the golden sunset light — one of the most vivid images in the story
Inaudible
Cannot be heard — too quiet to make out
Used when: her lips constantly moved in inaudible prayer. Formed by adding prefix "in-" to "audible." The textbook uses this as a prefix/suffix example

Literary devices are the tools a writer uses to make writing more powerful, more beautiful, more memorable. In the exam, you will be asked to identify and explain them. Here is every device in this story — with the exact line and a clear explanation.

Simile
"She was like the winter landscape in the mountains, an expanse of pure white serenity breathing peace and contentment."
A simile compares two things using "like" or "as." Here grandmother is compared to a snow-covered mountain landscape. The comparison works because both are white, both are vast, both are completely still and peaceful. It is one of the most beautiful similes in Indian English literature.
Metaphor
"The common link of friendship was snapped."
A metaphor compares without using "like" or "as." Here their friendship is compared to a thread or a link in a chain — and it is snapped. The word "snapped" makes you feel the sharpness of the break. Not loosened. Not frayed. Snapped.
Imagery — Visual
"Her face was a criss-cross of wrinkles running from everywhere to everywhere."
Imagery creates a vivid mental picture. "Criss-cross of wrinkles running from everywhere to everywhere" — you can see her face exactly. Not just "a wrinkled face." Every direction, every surface — covered in the map of a long life.
Imagery — Visual
"Thousands of sparrows sat scattered on the floor. There was no chirruping."
The contrast between thousands of birds and complete silence is deeply powerful. We expect noise from birds. The silence feels unnatural — and that unnaturalness is the mourning. This image stays with the reader long after the story ends.
Symbolism
The sparrows
The sparrows symbolise the grandmother's quiet, gentle companionship during her years of isolation. They are the only creatures who fully understand her worth. Their silence at her death and refusal to eat the bread is the story's most powerful symbolic moment — mourning expressed through action, not words.
Symbolism
The rosary
The rosary is with her in every phase of the story — always in her hand, always being counted. It symbolises her unbroken faith and her continuous connection with God. When it falls from her lifeless fingers, it signals not just her death but the end of that continuous prayer that had defined her entire life.
Symbolism
The spinning wheel
The spinning wheel symbolises her acceptance of her solitude and her rootedness in tradition. Just as Gandhi used the spinning wheel as a symbol of self-reliance and quiet dignity, grandmother's spinning wheel represents her quiet strength — she needs nothing and no one to sustain herself spiritually.
Tone
Throughout the story
The tone is gentle, nostalgic, and tender throughout. There is no anger, no blame — not at the parents for taking them to the city, not at himself for drifting away. Just a quiet sadness at how life moves forward and leaves some people behind. This restraint in tone makes the story feel honest rather than sentimental.
