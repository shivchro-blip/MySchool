About the Author

Let us begin with the man behind this wonderfully clever story. O. Henry was the pen name of William Sydney Porter, an American short-story writer who lived from 1862 to 1910. The textbook introduces him as a popular American writer, and that word “popular” is very important, because O. Henry did not write only for scholars. He wrote for ordinary readers who loved ordinary people, city streets, surprise endings, and stories that could be read with delight.

O. Henry’s education and early life are not explained in detail in the textbook, so we will not invent extra textbook facts here. What matters for our lesson is that his literary world is New York City. The textbook tells us that his works romanticized the life of ordinary people in New York City. That means he looked at the city not just as roads, shops, policemen, criminals, restaurants and street corners, but as a place where hidden emotions and unexpected decisions could suddenly appear.

His career as a writer became especially productive in New York. The textbook says that his prolific writing period began in 1902, and that he wrote 381 short stories. Just imagine that for a moment: one writer, hundreds of short stories, and many of them still remembered today because they end with a sudden turn that makes us rethink everything we have read.

What makes O. Henry distinctive? First, he is famous for coincidence. In “After Twenty Years,” one small action, Bob striking a match to light his cigar, allows Jimmy to recognize him. Second, he is famous for character revealed through a twist. Until the last note, we think we are reading only about a reunion. Then we realize we are reading about a policeman choosing duty over personal feeling. Third, the textbook says his stories are noted for witticism and clever wordplay. Notice the sharp sentence, “Twenty years is a long time, but not long enough to change a man’s nose from a Roman to a pug.” The humour and shock arrive together.

O. Henry’s story “After Twenty Years” was first published in the Sunday edition of the New York World in 1905. It was later included in the 1906 anthology The Four Million. The textbook also names some of his other popular short stories: The Gift of the Magi, The Cop and the Anthem, The Ransom of Red Chief, A Retrieved Reformation, and The Third Ingredient.

Why this matters for this text: Understanding O. Henry helps us read “After Twenty Years” properly. We must not treat the story as a simple friendship story. In O. Henry’s hands, a night street, a doorway, a policeman, and a waiting man become part of a careful trap. The author lets us believe one thing, then quietly turns the whole situation around. That is why every small detail in this story matters.

Introduction / Before We Read

Think of your closest friend. Suppose both of you promise to meet at the same place after twenty years, whatever happens in life. Would you go? And if you went, what would you expect to find there: the same friend, the same warmth, the same trust? Now let us make the question harder. What if your friend has changed into someone you cannot support? Would friendship still come first?

This is a short story, and a short story works like a tightly packed box. There is no room for waste. The weather, the street, the shop, the cigar, the diamond pin, the policeman’s questions, and the final note all have a purpose. When we read this story, we must watch details like a detective.

The textbook introduces the story by saying that two best friends had to part ways and return to keep an appointment they made twenty years ago. It also tells us the central conflict: Jimmy Wells is a true policeman torn between love for his friend and professional loyalty.

The policeman on the beat moved up the avenue impressively.

Notice how the opening begins not with Bob, but with the policeman. O. Henry places duty at the centre before friendship even enters the story. The word “impressively” shows us that this policeman is disciplined, alert, and respected. This line will appear in exams because it introduces the setting, the mood, and Jimmy Wells’s public identity as a guardian of peace.

The Text Explained

Phase 1 — The Night Beat

Let us first stand on that New York avenue with the policeman. It is barely ten o’clock at night, but the streets are almost empty because chilly gusts of wind carry the taste of rain. The policeman moves up the avenue, trying doors, twirling his club, and looking carefully down the peaceful street. This is not a lazy walk. This is a man doing his duty with alertness.

Trying doors as he went, twirling his club with many intricate and artful movements...

This line shows a policeman who is experienced and confident. The action of trying doors tells us he protects closed shops. The club-twirling gives him a slight swagger, but the textbook also says his impressiveness was habitual and not for show.

Notice thisThe story begins with duty. Before Bob talks about friendship, we are made to trust the policeman as a guardian of peace.

Phase 2 — The Waiting Man

Halfway down a block, the policeman notices a man leaning in the doorway of a dark hardware store. The man has an unlighted cigar in his mouth and quickly says he is only waiting for a friend. He explains that twenty years earlier, a restaurant named Big Joe Brady’s stood there, and he had dined there with Jimmy Wells, his best chum.

“It’s an appointment made twenty years ago. Sounds a little funny to you, doesn’t it?”

Bob knows the promise sounds unusual, so he explains before the officer suspects him. His words create curiosity. Is he loyal, foolish, sentimental, or hiding something?

When Bob lights his cigar, the match shows his face: pale, square-jawed, keen-eyed, with a little white scar near his right eyebrow. His diamond scarf pin suggests money and showiness. Bob proudly says he left for the West to make his fortune while Jimmy remained loyal to New York.

Think about thisThe match is a tiny action, but it changes the whole story. It lets Jimmy recognize the wanted criminal.

Phase 3 — Bob’s Faith in Jimmy

Bob speaks warmly about Jimmy. He says Jimmy was the truest, staunchest old chap in the world and would never forget. Bob has travelled a thousand miles to stand in that doorway. He takes out a handsome watch set with small diamonds and says it is three minutes to ten. His confidence reveals both loyalty and pride.

“I came a thousand miles to stand in this door tonight, and it’s worth it if my old partner turns up.”

This is one of the most exam-important lines because it shows Bob’s faith in old friendship. At the same time, his diamond watch and pin hint at questionable success. O. Henry lets admiration and suspicion stand side by side.

Bob also calls Jimmy a plodder and says the West put a razor-edge on him. This reveals his pride. He thinks he has become sharper than his old friend.

Phase 4 — The False Reunion

The policeman moves away, and Bob waits in the cold drizzle. After about twenty minutes, a tall man in a long overcoat crosses the street. He asks, “Is that you, Bob?” Bob thinks it is Jimmy Wells. They greet each other warmly and walk arm in arm. Bob talks about his success in the West while the other man listens.

“Bless my heart!” exclaimed the new arrival, grasping both the other’s hands with his own.

The scene feels emotional, but we must be careful. The tall man keeps his collar turned up. O. Henry hides his face so that Bob and the reader remain uncertain.

Notice thisThe false Jimmy says he has a position in one of the city departments. The statement is technically connected to policing, but Bob does not understand it yet.

Phase 5 — The Twist and the Note

At the corner, the electric lights of a drug store reveal both faces. Bob suddenly understands that the man is not Jimmy Wells. He says twenty years can change many things, but not a man’s nose from Roman to pug. Then comes the shock: the tall man is a plainclothes policeman, and Bob, known as “Silky” Bob, has been under arrest for ten minutes because Chicago wants him.

“It sometimes changes a good man into a bad one”, said the tall man.

This line is the moral turn of the story. Time has changed Bob, not physically into Jimmy, but morally from friend into criminal. The plainclothes man gives Bob a note from Patrolman Wells.

“Bob, I was at the appointed place on time. When you struck the match to light your cigar I saw it was the face of the man wanted in Chicago. Somehow I couldn’t do it myself, so I went around and got a plainclothes man to do the job.”

Here the whole story opens. Jimmy did come. Jimmy did recognize Bob. Jimmy still had feeling for his friend, but he could not betray his duty. This ending is powerful because it does not make Jimmy heartless. It shows a painful victory of professional loyalty over personal love.

Key Lines for the Exam

The policeman on the beat moved up the avenue impressively.

What it means: Jimmy Wells is patrolling his assigned area with authority. Exam tip: Character and setting. Key word: “impressively,” because it presents him as disciplined.

“I’m just waiting for a friend. It’s an appointment made twenty years ago.”

This explains Bob’s presence and introduces the promise. It is important for plot questions. The key word is “appointment,” because the whole story depends on that promise.

“He always was the truest, staunchest old chap in the world.”

Bob praises Jimmy’s loyalty. This line matters for character contrast. “staunchest” is the key word because it means firm and dependable.

“I came a thousand miles to stand in this door tonight...”

Bob’s effort shows faith in friendship, but also dramatic irony because he does not know Jimmy is already there. “thousand” intensifies his commitment.

“Twenty years is a long time, but not long enough to change a man’s nose from a Roman to a pug.”

Bob realizes the tall man is not Jimmy. It is important for the turning point. The contrast “Roman to a pug” is humorous comparison and sharp observation.

“It sometimes changes a good man into a bad one.”

The plainclothes man states the moral truth of the story. The key word is “changes,” because the story asks what time can and cannot change.

“Somehow I couldn’t do it myself...”

Jimmy’s note reveals his emotional conflict. It matters for duty versus friendship. “couldn’t” is the key word because it shows he is human, not cruel.

Themes and Literary Devices

Friendship tested by time

The story shows that friendship may remain emotionally powerful even after twenty years, but time also reveals what people have become. Bob remembers Jimmy as the finest chap in the world and travels a thousand miles to meet him. Jimmy also keeps the appointment, but he finds not merely an old friend; he finds a wanted man. In an essay, write that O. Henry presents friendship as sincere but not strong enough to erase moral responsibility.

Duty versus personal feeling

Jimmy Wells is the heart of this theme. He comes on time, recognizes Bob, and still cannot arrest him with his own hands. The note says, “Somehow I couldn’t do it myself,” which proves he is torn between affection and duty. For exam writing, argue that Jimmy’s greatness lies not in rejecting friendship, but in refusing to misuse friendship to protect crime.

Appearance and identity

The story is full of appearances: Bob’s diamond pin, handsome watch, scar, the tall man’s raised collar, and the final change from shadow to electric light. Bob appears successful, but he is wanted by Chicago police. The tall man appears to be Jimmy, but is a plainclothes officer. O. Henry uses appearance to make us ask whether people are truly what they seem.

Literary devices

Irony: Bob waits for Jimmy while unknowingly speaking to Jimmy at the beginning. This is effective because the reader discovers the truth only at the end. Foreshadowing: Bob’s diamond pin, his pride in the West, and Jimmy’s questions hint that something is hidden. Symbolism: The match symbolizes revelation because its light exposes Bob’s face. Contrast: Roman nose and pug nose create the moment of recognition and comic shock.
