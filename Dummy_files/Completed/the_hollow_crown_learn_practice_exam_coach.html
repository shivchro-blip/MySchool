About the Poet and Context

William Shakespeare is one of the greatest dramatists and poets in English literature. The textbook gives this poem as an extract from his historical play Richard II. The full name note in the textbook says that Shakespeare’s full name is William Arden Shakespeare, where Arden is his mother’s family name.

This passage is a monologue spoken by King Richard II when he is defeated and forced to face the loss of his power. In it, the king understands that the power of Death overshadows everything, including royal power. Kings may wear crowns, command armies and receive reverence, but they are still made of flesh and blood like common people.

Why this matters for this text: The poem is not merely about one defeated king. It teaches an eternal truth: worldly power is temporary, and death makes rulers and subjects equal. Richard’s grief becomes wisdom because he sees that the crown is hollow when death sits within it.

Before We Read

The warm-up asks students to think about wars, battles, why rulers fight, whether war outcomes are fair, and whether rulers understand life better in defeat or victory. This prepares us for Richard, a ruler who learns the truth of life only after losing his crown.

In it, King Richard II is reminded of the power of Death that overshadows everything else, including the power of rulers, and renders them as powerless as any commoner at a moment’s notice.

This textbook introduction gives the central idea. The poem is about the fall of royal pride and the rise of human truth.
